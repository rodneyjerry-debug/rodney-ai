### The lever nobody wants to talk about at the board table

Parts 1 and 2 of this series were about growth — new fee pools, new asset classes, new revenue lines. Part 3 is about something less glamorous and arguably more urgent: the capital you already have, and how much of it is being wasted.

Capital efficiency is the quiet lever. It doesn't generate headlines the way tokenised sukuks or embedded finance do. But in a sector where the aggregate balance sheet is growing faster than equity can replenish, it may be the lever that determines which Saudi banks can actually fund the Vision 2030 pipeline — and which ones hit a wall.

The Scoreboard puts the Capital Efficiency lever at **SAR 0.5B (Low) to SAR 5.3B (High)** in annual value, with a Base case of **SAR 2.5B**. That's not revenue. It's released capital — capacity that can be redeployed into the growth levers from Parts 1 and 2 without issuing a single new share or AT1 sukuk.

The question is whether Saudi banks treat this as a treasury housekeeping exercise or a strategic programme with a named owner, a target number, and a date.

---

### 01 · The capital squeeze is already here

Saudi banks are well capitalised. The sector-wide Capital Adequacy Ratio stood at 19.2% in September 2024, comfortably above SAMA's minimums. Individual banks like Al Rajhi are running CARs above 21%. The numbers look healthy.

But look at the trajectory. SAMA has announced a countercyclical capital buffer of 1% effective May 2026. Basel III Final Reforms — what the industry calls Basel IV — are being phased in through 2028, with output floors rising by five percentage points annually until they hit 72.5%. And Saudi bank balance sheets are expanding aggressively to fund Vision 2030's infrastructure, housing, and giga-project pipeline.

The arithmetic is simple: **capital demand is growing faster than capital supply**. Every SAR of capital that is locked up in an inefficient risk-weight is a SAR that cannot be deployed into the growth opportunities this series has been sizing.

This is not a solvency conversation. It is a capacity conversation.

---

### 02 · Three forces reshaping the capital stack

**Force 1: Output floors bite harder than you think**

The Basel IV output floor requires banks using internal ratings-based (IRB) models to hold capital equal to at least 72.5% of what the standardised approach would demand. For banks with sophisticated internal models, this narrows the capital benefit of IRB. For banks still on the standardised approach, the impact is different but no less real — it raises the bar for what "good enough" modelling looks like.

In Europe, the estimated impact is an 18.5% average increase in RWAs, with a total capital shortfall of approximately EUR 52 billion across the sector. The ECB's Targeted Review of Internal Models (TRIM) identified over 5,000 deficiencies across European banks and added approximately EUR 275 billion in risk-weighted assets.

Saudi banks will feel this differently. Most Saudi banks operate primarily on the standardised approach, which means the direct output floor impact is smaller — but the opportunity cost is larger. Every Saudi bank that is *not* on IRB is carrying a higher risk-weight than its portfolio actually warrants. The floor creates an incentive to move *towards* IRB, not away from it — the opposite of what European banks are experiencing.

**Force 2: AT1 sukuk issuance is accelerating — and that's a signal**

Saudi banks issued $4.2 billion in Additional Tier 1 sukuk in 2025, more than double the $2 billion in the same period of 2024. Al Rajhi, Saudi Investment Bank, Bank AlJazira, Saudi Awwal Bank — the issuance pipeline is broad-based.

This is not banks being conservative. This is banks recognising that organic capital generation alone cannot keep pace with balance sheet growth. Fitch projects $10 billion in Saudi bank sukuk issuance in 2026.

AT1 sukuk are a valid tool. But they are also expensive capital. Every SAR of released capital from RWA optimisation is a SAR you didn't need to raise externally — at a coupon you didn't need to pay.

**Force 3: Credit growth is outrunning capital formation**

The Saudi banking sector's ROE strengthened to 15.2% in FY2025, with a cost-to-income ratio of 29.5% in Q2 2025 — world-class operational efficiency. But the loan book is growing at double digits to support Vision 2030 financing. The gap between asset growth and retained earnings accumulation is widening.

Banks that can run a leaner risk-weight per unit of exposure will compound that advantage across every lending decision, every quarter, for the rest of the decade.

### 03 · The four moves that release capital

The Scoreboard model decomposes the Capital Efficiency lever into four distinct optimisation pathways. None requires a single new product or customer. All operate on the existing balance sheet.

**Move 1: IRB migration and model sophistication**

Saudi banks that migrate from the standardised approach to foundation IRB (F-IRB) for their core credit portfolios can expect meaningful RWA reductions. Global benchmarks suggest RWA density improvements of 5–15 percentage points on well-collateralised corporate and mortgage books.

This is where AI and machine learning enter the capital efficiency story. Neural network-based credit risk models have been shown to deliver the strongest RWA reductions among AI techniques, primarily because they capture non-linear risk relationships that traditional scorecards miss. The challenge is regulatory acceptance — SAMA and any supervisor will demand explainability, validation under SR 11-7 principles, and conceptual soundness. But the direction of travel is clear: banks with better models hold less capital for the same portfolio.

**Move 2: Collateral optimisation and data quality**

European banks that have addressed poor collateral data quality have reported savings of EUR 20 million per annum in released capital — driven entirely by more accurate recognition of eligible collateral in the RWA calculation.

Saudi banks hold some of the richest collateral bases in the region — government-backed guarantees, real estate in appreciating markets, investment-grade securities. The question is whether the systems that calculate risk weights are capturing the full value of that collateral. In many cases, they are not.

**Move 3: Securitisation and balance sheet recycling**

A bank that originates SAR 10 billion in mortgage exposure at a 35% standardised risk weight is consuming SAR 3.5 billion of RWA. If that same exposure is securitised into a Significant Risk Transfer (SRT) structure, the originating bank can reduce the capital charge to a fraction of the original — while retaining servicing income and customer relationships.

Saudi Arabia's securitisation market is nascent but developing. The Saudi Real Estate Refinancing Company (SRC) has been building the secondary mortgage market infrastructure. As SAMA's regulatory framework matures, synthetic and traditional securitisation will become a mainstream capital management tool.

**Move 4: Portfolio-level risk-weight optimisation**

Not all assets are created equal from a capital consumption perspective. A bank that actively manages the composition of its balance sheet — tilting towards exposures with lower risk weights per unit of expected return — compounds the capital efficiency benefit across the entire book.

This is where the Ontology-First AI approach becomes load-bearing. Building a knowledge graph that maps every exposure to its risk weight, collateral coverage, expected loss, and strategic value allows a bank to make portfolio allocation decisions that are simultaneously credit-sound and capital-efficient. Without this kind of structured reasoning, portfolio optimisation is a spreadsheet exercise. With it, it's a continuous, AI-driven process.

### 04 · The scoreboard: Capital Efficiency lever

| Pathway | Low | Base | High |
|---------|-----|------|------|
| IRB migration & model sophistication | 0.1 | 0.8 | 2.0 |
| Collateral optimisation & data quality | 0.1 | 0.5 | 0.8 |
| Securitisation & balance sheet recycling | 0.2 | 0.7 | 1.5 |
| Portfolio risk-weight optimisation | 0.1 | 0.5 | 1.0 |
| **TOTAL · SAR B / year** | **0.5** | **2.5** | **5.3** |

The Low scenario assumes banks do nothing beyond the bare minimum to comply with Basel IV. The Base case assumes a coordinated programme across at least two of the four pathways. The High case assumes an institution-wide capital efficiency transformation — IRB migration, AI-powered modelling, active securitisation, and continuous portfolio optimisation — run as a strategic programme with board-level sponsorship.

---

### 05 · What separates a Base case bank from a High case bank

A Base case bank treats capital efficiency as a regulatory compliance exercise. It has a Basel IV programme. It has a capital planning team. It runs stress tests. It meets the numbers.

A High case bank treats capital as a strategic resource. It has a Chief Capital Officer — or equivalent — who sits in the same room as the Chief AI Officer and the Chief Risk Officer. It uses AI not just to score credit risk, but to optimise the risk-return-capital equation across every new origination. It securitises not because it needs liquidity, but because recycling capital is a higher-ROE strategy than holding to maturity. It runs a live portfolio optimisation engine that rebalances risk weights quarterly.

The difference between SAR 0.5B and SAR 5.3B per year is not technology. It is governance. It is a board that understands that every basis point of RWA density is a resource allocation decision — and treats it accordingly.

---

### 06 · The connection to Levers 1 and 2

Capital efficiency is not an end in itself. It is the enabler of the other three levers in the Scoreboard.

The Monetization play from Part 1 — SAR 29.5B in new fee income — requires investment in BaaS platforms, data products, and API infrastructure. That investment consumes capital.

The Tokenization play from Part 2 — regulated DLT venues, sukuk on-chain — requires new systems, new risk frameworks, and new regulatory capital allocations. That consumes capital too.

Every SAR released through the Capital Efficiency lever is a SAR available to fund Levers 1, 2, and 4 — without dilution, without external issuance, without coupon payments.

The Scoreboard is not four independent levers. It is a system. And capital efficiency is the circulation system that feeds the others.
